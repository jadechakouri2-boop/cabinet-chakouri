Morocco Horizon 2030

October 9, 20268 min read

Morocco will co-host the 2030 World Cup with Spain and Portugal. Centered on this event, it is carrying out an investment program that the IMF puts at approximately MAD 190 billion. Stadiums have been delivered, a high-speed line is under construction, industry is exporting: the opportunities are here now, provided you distinguish what is already secured from what remains to be done.

The 2030 World Cup, an accelerator

According to the IMF, investment linked to the tournament amounts to approximately MAD 190 billion, or 11.9% of 2024 GDP. Rail is the largest item (6% of GDP), ahead of airports, stadiums and highways. The Fund expects GDP to be about 2% higher in 2030.

Completed. The Rabat stadium (68,700 seats) was designed and built in 24 months. In Tangier, the renovated Grand Stade (75,600 seats) received a new roof, installed in 69 days. AFCON 2025 drew more than 1.25 million spectators and generated $192.6 million in revenue, according to CAF.

Under construction. The Grand Stade Hassan II in Benslimane (115,000 seats planned) was about 30% complete on May 25, 2026, according to ANEP. Delivery is targeted for the end of 2027, and several contract lots have yet to be awarded, including the one for seating.

According to the IMF, about 60% of spending would go to imports, notably high-speed trains and airport equipment. Foreign suppliers therefore have a place, especially alongside Moroccan partners: five Moroccan companies won the access road project for the Grand Stade (a contract estimated at MAD 605 million). Financing, 67% of which would rely on domestic bank lending, also opens the way to project finance and bond issues.

World-class infrastructure

Rail. The rail program amounts to MAD 96 billion through 2030, including MAD 53 billion for the Kenitra–Marrakech high-speed line and MAD 29 billion for 168 trains. The line was nearly 30% complete in May 2026; delivery is expected between September and the end of 2029. The Rabat–Marrakech journey will then take 1 hour 40 minutes. Nearly two-thirds of the roughly 150 companies working on the project are Moroccan. For the extension to Agadir (about MAD 55 billion), the studies are complete, but financing has yet to be found.

Ports. Tanger Med handled 11.1 million TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units) in 2025; it is the world’s 16th-largest container port according to Lloyd’s List. The East Terminal of Nador West Med (3.4 million TEU of capacity) has been ready since September 2026 to receive its first port calls. The Dakhla Atlantique port was more than 60% complete in June 2026.

Airports. Traffic reached a record 36.3 million passengers in 2025 (+11%). A MAD 38 billion agreement covering 2025-2030 earmarks 25 billion of it for a new terminal and a new runway at Casablanca Mohammed V, as well as for the expansion of the Marrakech, Agadir, Tangier and Fez airports. According to Le Desk, ONDA is targeting a capacity of 80 million passengers a year in 2030.

Green energy and water

At the end of 2025, renewables accounted for 46.1% of installed electricity capacity. The target is to exceed 52% in 2030.

ONEE’s infrastructure plan, adopted on July 27, 2026, provides for MAD 177 billion for electricity over 2026-2030. It includes MAD 104 billion for 11.6 GW of new renewable capacity, as well as 2.6 GW of storage. The decisive point: 72% of the total is to be financed directly by the private sector. Masen is already building Noor Atlas (305 MW of solar), with commissioning starting in July 2027.

In green hydrogen, five investors were selected in 2025 for six projects totaling about MAD 319 billion. These projects are at the study stage, with no final investment decision.

Desalination is scaling up: 410 million m³ per year in 2026 according to the outgoing government, with more than 1.7 billion targeted for 2030. The Greater Casablanca plant (MAD 6.5 billion, as a public-private partnership) is due to commission its first phase on February 1, 2027. Signed in May 2025 with Taqa Morocco, Nareva and the Mohammed VI Investment Fund, agreements worth about MAD 130 billion cover, in particular, desalination, water transfer and green electricity.

An industrial base that attracts the giants

Automotive is the leading export sector: MAD 154.7 billion in 2025 (−1.8%), then +14.5% year on year at the end of August 2026. Renault, which produced more than 394,000 vehicles in Morocco in 2025, is targeting 80% local integration by 2030. On July 15, 2026, in Kenitra, Stellantis launched production of the Fiat Grizzly and Fastback on its “Smart Car” platform. The Ministry of Industry announces more than €6 billion in Stellantis purchases from Moroccan suppliers by 2030.

The aerospace sector exported MAD 29.1 billion in 2025 (+10%). In Nouaceur, Safran is building a LEAP engine assembly and maintenance complex, as well as a landing gear plant. The sector has an integration rate of about 42% and is targeting 60% in 2030: there is real room for suppliers.

A battery industry is emerging. COBCO has been producing cathode precursors at Jorf Lasfar since June 2025. In Kenitra, the AfDB approved a €100 million loan in July 2026 for Gotion’s gigafactory, whose production, announced for 2026, has not yet been confirmed.

Net FDI inflows reached MAD 34.3 billion at the end of August 2026 (+65%, provisional figure), already more than for the whole of 2025. However, the drop in divestments accounts for part of this increase. France remains the leading foreign investor.

An incentive-based investment framework

The Investment Charter grants subsidies capped at 30% of eligible investment: subsidies linked in particular to employment, local integration and sustainable development, a territorial subsidy and a sectoral subsidy. From MAD 2 billion upward, a project may, subject to conditions, obtain strategic status and a negotiated agreement. A dedicated scheme has covered VSMEs since July 2025. Since 2023, 391 agreements have been approved, representing MAD 520 billion in planned investment.

The corporate income tax rate is 20% below MAD 100 million of net profit (35% above that). Industrial acceleration zones and Casablanca Finance City status offer a five-year exemption, followed by a 20% rate. The Mohammed VI Investment Fund can act as a co-investor, notably through its 14 sectoral and thematic funds.

The macroeconomic framework is stable. Growth reached 4.9% in 2025. Inflation averaged 0.3% over the first eight months of 2026, and the key policy rate remains at 2.25%. S&P has rated Morocco “investment grade” since September 2025; Moody’s, which has Morocco on a positive outlook, and Fitch do not yet rate it as such. In May 2026, the Treasury raised €2.25 billion, against demand of 5.2 billion.

The gateway to Africa

The European Union accounts for 33.7% of Morocco’s trade in goods. The country is also linked to the United States by the only US free trade agreement in Africa (in force since 2006), and it signed the AfCFTA in 2018.

To the south, Moroccan banks have 1,520 branches in 27 African countries. Casablanca Finance City is Africa’s No. 1 financial center and ranks 38th worldwide in the September 2026 GFCI: a natural base for a regional headquarters. In July 2026, ECOWAS heads of state signed the intergovernmental agreement for the African Atlantic gas pipeline, which is to link Nigeria to Morocco. Still outstanding at that point were the signatures of Morocco and Mauritania, then the final investment decision.

Tourism and digital

Morocco welcomed 19.8 million tourists in 2025 (+14%), then 15.7 million at the end of September 2026 (+5%), a sign of slowing growth. The target is 26 million in 2030. To this end, the country plans about 60,000 additional beds, on top of a stock of just over 300,000. Office real estate is also on the move: transactions rose 36.7% year on year in the second quarter of 2026.

Digital Morocco 2030 targets 240,000 direct jobs and 3,000 startups. Offshoring, which accounted for 148,500 jobs at the end of 2024, is targeting 270,000 in 2030, with incentives in force until the end of 2030; telesales, for their part, have been exposed since August 11, 2026, to the French law on telephone cold calling. The country is also targeting nearly 1 GW of data centers, even though the first phase of the Nouaceur project has been postponed.

Points to watch

  • Costs. The official budget for the Grand Stade Hassan II is about MAD 5 billion, but Le Desk counts 13.6 billion in contracts awarded or launched. The IMF forecasts public debt to rise by about 8 points of GDP by 2030.

  • Sporting unknowns. The venue for the final has not been awarded, the format (48 or 64 teams) is under review, and the AFCON 2025 title, awarded to Morocco by CAF, awaits the CAS award, under deliberation since the hearing of October 8, 2026.

  • The US market. Since July 24, 2026, an additional 12.5% duty has applied to Moroccan products. Civil aircraft, passenger vehicles and their parts are notably excluded.

  • The political transition. Appointed Head of Government on September 29, 2026, Fatima-Zahra El Mansouri is forming her team, which will have to confirm its priorities.

The sectors to target by 2030

The needs have been identified. Construction and engineering firms will find contracts in stadiums, rail and ports. Rail and airport equipment manufacturers will tap into spending that goes largely to imports. Renewable electricity, storage and desalination are opening up to private capital. Automotive and aerospace subcontracting, like the battery supply chain, offers outlets for Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers. Hospitality and commercial real estate will keep pace with rising arrivals. Data centers and high-value-added offshoring round out the list. Project finance and public-private partnerships will be at the heart of deal structures.
